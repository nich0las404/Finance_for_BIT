Income Statement

Learn how balance sheets, income statements, and cash flows communicate business health and financial performance over time.

Income Statement

The Income Statement (often called the Profit and Loss statement, or P&L) is a financial report that shows a company's financial performance over a specific period of time, such as a month, quarter, or year.

The major line items of the Income Statement are the earnings and expenses line items, showing how revenue flows through operating costs down to net income.

Earnings & Expenses

We often hear a business earns money, but what type of money has it actually generated? It turns out that there are multiple earnings line items such as Revenue, Gross Profit, EBITDA, EBIT, and Net Profit.

It's the same with costs: COGS, Operational Expenses (SG&A), Interest Expense, and Tax Expense. Here we breakdown what each earnings (inflow) and expenses (outflow) line item means.

Imagine you are running a lemonade stand business, and after your first month you've received $500 through selling your lemonade drinks. That $500 sitting in your cash register represents your Revenue.

💡 Revenue (or Sales) = the total amount of money coming in from selling your drinks before subtracting any costs whatsoever.

To figure out how much you actually earned, you have to start paying off your costs. First up are your direct ingredients: lemons, sugar, ice, and paper cups. Over the month, these ingredients cost you $150. In accounting, this is your COGS.

💡 Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) = the direct expenses required to produce the product you sell.

When you subtract your COGS from your Revenue ($500 – $150), you are left with $350 in Gross Profit. This metric shows how efficiently your core product is priced relative to its direct material costs.

Gross Profit = Revenue - COGS

However, making lemonade isn't your only expense. To keep the business running, you spent $50 on wooden advertising signs and paid a friend $50 to stand by the road and call out to customers. These are your Operational Expenses (SG&A). They aren't part of the physical lemonade, but they are necessary to run day-to-day operations.

💡 SG&A (Selling, General, and Administrative) = costs related to managing daily business operations, excluding direct production expenses.

Subtracting your SG&A from your Gross Profit ($350 – $100) leaves you with $250 in EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization). This number reflects the raw cash generation power of your lemonade operations, before taking into account accounting rules or financing structures.

💡 EBITDA = Revenue - COGS - SG&A

Now, let's say you bought a high-tech electric lemon squeezer for $300 at the start of the month, which is expected to last for three months. Instead of taking a massive $300 hit in month one, accounting rules let you spread that expense out over its useful life. There, you record $100 per month as Depreciation.

💡 Depreciation = an accounting method used to spread out the cost of a tangible asset over its useful life, reflecting the loss in value from wear and tear
(note: for ease of explanation, amortization is similar to depreciation but for intangible/ non-physical assets)

Subtracting this non-cash expense from your EBITDA ($250 – $100) brings you to $150 in EBIT (Earnings Before Interest and Taxes), also known as your Operating Profit. This measures your fundamental business profitability, including the wear and tear on your equipment.

💡 EBIT/ Operating Profit = Revenue - COGS - SG&A - D&A

There are still two other major expenses that needs to be noted which are:

- Interest Expense: To buy the lemonade stand structure, you took out a small loan from a parent and now owe $10 in interest for the month. Subtracting this leaves you with $140.
- Tax Expense: The local student entrepreneur club charges a 10% tax on your remaining earnings, taking $14.

Subtracting those two from EBIT gives us the final earnings line item which is Net Profit.

💡 Net Profit = EBIT - Interest Expense - Tax Expense

This final line item will flow to the other two financial statements (Balance Sheet and Cash Flow Statement).

Accrual Accounting Method

You might be wondering: “Wait, when did I actually earn the $50 from the customer who promised to pay me tomorrow?” This is where the Accrual Accounting Method comes in. Instead of tracking money only when it physically enters your pocket (cash basis), accrual accounting recognizes revenue when it is earned, and expenses when they are incurred.

At its core, the choice between accrual accounting and cash-based accounting comes down to timing. Cash-based accounting is straightforward: you record transactions only when physical dollars move in or out of your lemonade stand's cash register. In contrast, accrual accounting tracks economic activity regardless of when cash changes hands, logging revenues when they are earned and expenses when they are incurred. While cash accounting works for managing a simple personal wallet, accrual accounting serves as the standard framework for serious businesses because it reflects real operational activity rather than misleading cash fluctuations.

The Revenue Recognition Principle dictates that you must record revenue in the exact period when a good or service is delivered to a customer, not when they hand over the cash. Imagine a neighbor orders $30 worth of lemonade for a party on May 31st. You deliver all 10 cups that afternoon, but they forget their wallet and promise to pay you next week. Under accrual accounting, that $30 is recorded as May revenue because the work was fulfilled in May. Under cash accounting, your May sales would show $0 simply because the bills haven't entered your register yet.

Complementing this is the Matching Principle, which mandates that all costs incurred to generate a specific batch of revenue must be recorded in the exact same time period as that revenue. Suppose you buy $50 worth of lemons and sugar at the end of May, but you don't actually mix and sell the lemonade until June. Under cash accounting, May takes a heavy $50 hit while June gets free ingredients. Accrual accounting fixes this: the $50 sits safely on your balance sheet as inventory during May, and only converts into a $50 Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) in June, matching the lemons directly to the sales they produced.

Understanding these concepts matters because relying solely on cash-based accounting distorts the financial reality of your lemonade stand. Under cash accounting, your business might look artificially rich in May if a customer prepays $100 for a July catering gig, or it might look near bankruptcy because you paid $300 upfront for an electric lemon squeezer expected to last three months. Accrual accounting cuts through the noise of cash timing, ensuring managers, investors, and analysts see an accurate evaluation of actual business performance and long-term viability.

💡 Revenue Recognition Principle = Record revenue when earned (service delivered), not when cash is received.

Matching Principle = Record expenses in the same period as the revenue they helped generate.

Margin Analysis

Raw dollar amounts don't tell the full story of a business. A company generating $1,000,000 in net profit might sound impressive, but if it required $100,000,000 in revenue to get there, its profit margin is only 1%. A single unexpected cost could push it into the red.

Margin Analysis converts dollar figures into percentages, allowing you to evaluate how efficiently a business converts revenue into profit, regardless of whether it is a small lemonade stand or a multinational enterprise.

Margin % = (Earnings/Revenue) * 100

Now, let's use the income of our lemonade stand to demonstrate the margin analysis.

Gross Profit Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue
Calculation: $250 / $500 = 50%
What it means: Measures raw operational cash profitability before non-cash accounting adjustments (like depreciation) and capital structure decisions (interest and taxes). It evaluates the operational efficiency of the core business model.

Operating Profit Margin (EBIT Margin) = EBIT / Revenue
Calculation: $150 / $500 = 30%
What it means: Shows what percentage of revenue remains after accounting for both direct production costs and day-to-day administrative overhead, including equipment wear-and-tear. It measures how effectively management operates the business.

Net Profit Margin = Net Income / Revenue
Calculation: $126 / $500 = 25.2%
What it means: The ultimate "bottom line" metric. It reveals how many cents of every revenue dollar end up as pure profit for the business owners after all operational expenses, lender interest, and government taxes are paid.
