Enterprise Value to Equity Value Bridge

Learn how to translate the total value of a business's operations into the actual value belonging to its shareholders, completing the final step of a DCF valuation.

Enterprise Value to Equity Value Bridge

When you build a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model using Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) and discount it by the WACC, the result is the Enterprise Value (EV). This represents the total value of the company's core business operations, regardless of how it is financed.

However, investors don't buy "Enterprise Value"; they buy shares of stock. To find out what those shares are actually worth, we must build a "bridge" from Enterprise Value down to Equity Value.

The Core Formula: The Bridge

Enterprise Value represents the value of the business to all capital providers (both debt and equity holders). To isolate the value that belongs strictly to the common shareholders, we must account for the company's non-operating assets and financial obligations.

💡 Equity Value = Enterprise Value + Cash & Cash Equivalents - Total Debt - Preferred Stock - Minority Interest

Why add Cash? Cash is a non-operating asset. It doesn't generate operating cash flow, but it belongs to the shareholders. If you buy the whole company, you get its cash.

Why subtract Debt? Debt holders have a senior claim on the company's assets. Before equity holders get a single dollar, all debt must be paid off. Therefore, debt reduces the value available to shareholders.

Real-World Corporate Finance Example: The "House" Analogy

The easiest way to understand this bridge is to think about buying a house.

Suppose a house has a market value of $500,000. This is the Enterprise Value of the property.

However, the current owner still has a $300,000 mortgage (Debt) on the house. Additionally, when you buy the house, you also get a safe in the basement containing $20,000 in cash (Cash).

How much cash do you actually need to pay the seller to acquire the house? $500,000 (House Value) - $300,000 (Mortgage) + $20,000 (Cash in safe) = $220,000.

💡 Result: The $220,000 is the Equity Value. In corporate finance, if you acquire a company, you pay the Equity Value, but you effectively assume control of the Enterprise Value and its associated debt and cash.

From Equity Value to Share Price

Once you have calculated the total Equity Value, the final step in a DCF model is to divide this number by the fully diluted share count (the total number of shares outstanding, including stock options and warrants).

Intrinsic Share Price = Equity Value / Fully Diluted Shares Outstanding

Analysts then compare this intrinsic share price to the company's current trading price on the stock market. If the intrinsic price is higher, the stock is considered undervalued (a "Buy"). If it is lower, the stock is overvalued (a "Sell").
