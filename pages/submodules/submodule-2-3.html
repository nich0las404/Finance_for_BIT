Capital Structure & Cost of Debt

Learn how companies finance their operations through a mix of debt and equity, and how to calculate the true cost of borrowing after accounting for tax benefits.

Capital Structure & Cost of Debt

Capital structure refers to the specific mix of debt and equity a company uses to finance its overall operations and future growth.

Finding the right balance is crucial. Too much debt increases bankruptcy risk, while relying solely on equity can dilute ownership and be more expensive in the long run.

The Mix: Debt vs. Equity

Every dollar a company uses to fund its business comes from either creditors (debt) or shareholders (equity). Understanding the difference is key to managing corporate finances.

💡 Debt = Borrowed funds (like bank loans or corporate bonds) that must be repaid with interest. Equity = Ownership stakes (like common stock or retained earnings) that do not require mandatory repayments.

Debt is generally cheaper than equity because it carries less risk for the investor (they get paid first in bankruptcy). However, debt comes with the strict obligation to make regular interest payments.

The True Cost of Borrowing (After-Tax Cost of Debt)

When calculating the cost of debt, corporate finance managers don't just look at the interest rate the bank charges. They look at the after-tax cost of debt.

This is because interest expenses are tax-deductible. The government effectively subsidizes a portion of a company's borrowing costs, a concept known as the "interest tax shield."

💡 After-Tax Cost of Debt = Pre-Tax Interest Rate × (1 - Corporate Tax Rate)

Real-World Corporate Finance Example

Suppose Company A borrows $1,000,000 from a bank at a 6% annual interest rate. The corporate tax rate is 25%.

Pre-Tax Interest Expense = $1,000,000 × 6% = $60,000.
Tax Savings (Tax Shield) = $60,000 × 25% = $15,000.

After-Tax Cost of Debt = 6% × (1 - 0.25) = 4.5%.

💡 Result: Even though the bank charges 6%, the true cost to the company is only 4.5% because the interest expense reduces the company's taxable income, saving them $15,000 in taxes.

How Corporate Finance Uses Capital Structure Every Day

WACC Calculation: Financial analysts use the cost of debt and cost of equity to calculate the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC), which serves as the primary discount rate for valuing new projects and acquisitions.

Leverage & Risk Management: CFOs constantly monitor the Debt-to-Equity ratio to ensure the company isn't over-leveraged, which could lead to financial distress during economic downturns.

Credit Ratings: Companies structure their debt carefully to maintain favorable credit ratings from agencies like Moody's or S&P, which directly lowers their future cost of borrowing.
