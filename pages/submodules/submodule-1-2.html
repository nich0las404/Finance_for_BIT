Submodule 1.2

Balance Sheet

A balance sheet is a financial statement that provides a snapshot of a company's assets, liabilities, and owner's equity at a specific point in time.

Balance Sheet

Distinguishing between current assets (cash, receivables, inventory) vs. long-term assets, and short-term obligations vs. long-term debt.

The sun hits your wooden sign at 8:00 AM on June 1st. Yesterday, Month 1 came to a close, and you wrote down your final Income Statement. You made $126 in Net Profit.

Now, you step back and look at the physical setup. You aren't just looking at how much money came in or went out last month, but everything your business has, everything it owes, and what it’s actually worth at this exact second.

While the Income Statement is like a video showing performance over time, the Balance Sheet is a snapshot, a single photograph of your financial position at one specific moment.

It is governed by one fundamental equation that must always balance:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Assets & Liabilities

1. Assets (What Your Own Stand Owns)
You open up your cash register and look across your stand. Everything you see that brings future economic value to your business is an Asset.

* Cash ($386): Inside the register, you count $386 in crisp bills. This comes from your monthly sales after paying off ingredients, advertising, interest, and taxes, plus the $30 your neighbor owed you from May that they dropped off first thing this morning.

* Accounts Receivable ($30): On the wall, there's a sticky note: "Catering tab for Mrs. Higgins → $30." She bought 10 cups on credit yesterday. You haven't received the cash yet, but she will pay. That promise has real economic value.

* Inventory ($50): On the bottom shelf sit three unused bags of sugar, a carton of fresh lemons, and a stack of paper cups bought last month. They haven't been used yet, so they sit on your Balance Sheet as $50 of inventory waiting to become future sales.

* Property, Plant, and Equipment / PP&E ($200): Right in the center sits your heavy-duty electric lemon squeezer. You paid $300 for it on day one. But after a month of grinding through hundreds of lemons, it has experienced some wear and tear. You subtract $100 in Accumulated Depreciation, leaving its net book value at $200.

2. Liabilities (What Your Stand Owes)
You open a small drawer under the counter. Inside are the obligations you owe to outside parties.

* Accounts Payable ($40): An invoice from the local ice supplier. You took delivery of $40 worth of crushed ice on credit two days ago, and the bill is due next week.

* Bank / Loan ($500): The original $500 loan you took out to build the wooden stand structure and buy initial equipment.

Add up your obligations:

💡 Total Liabilities = $40 (Payable) + $500 (Loan) = $540

Equity Components

Now comes the ultimate question: If you sold off all your assets today for $666 in cash and paid off every single debt of $540, what would actually belong to you as the owner?

That remainder is your Equity (or Net Worth):

* Contributed Capital ($0): You started the stand with $0 of your own savings, financing the launch entirely through the initial loan.
* Retained Earnings ($126): Here is where the magic happens. The $126 Net Profit you earned on your Income Statement at the end of Month 1 flows directly into Retained Earnings on your Balance Sheet. Your hard work over the past 30 days built $126 of real value into the business.
* Treasury Stock ($0): At the end of your first month, you haven't bought back any shares or ownership stakes from partners yet, so this subtraction stays at zero.

💡 Total Equity = $0 (Contributed Capital) + $126 (Retained Earnings) - $0 (Treasury Stock) = $126

1. Equity Deep Dive: How the Owner's Stake Grows & Changes
As your lemonade stand expands beyond Month 1, Equity will change in three distinct ways depending on where the money comes from and where it goes.

* Share Capital (Contributed Capital)
This is the money brought into the business from the outside by owners investing their personal savings.

Imagine on Day 1 of Month 2, you want to scale up operations faster than your lemonade sales alone allow. You bring in a partner who invests $100 of their own money into the cash register in exchange for a 20% ownership stake in the stand.

* Contributed Capital increases from $0 to $100.
* This isn't revenue earned from selling lemonade, it is external equity capital injected directly by an owner to build the business foundation.

2. Retained Earnings (Earned Capital)
While Share Capital is external money put into the business, Retained Earnings are internal profits generated by the business and kept inside to fund future growth.

This is the direct bridge connecting your Income Statement to your Balance Sheet:

Ending Retained Earnings = Beginning Retained Earnings + Net Profit - Dividends

Suppose in Month 2: * You start with $126 in Beginning Retained Earnings from Month 1.
* Your stand generates another $150 in Net Profit on Month 2's Income Statement.
* You decide to pay yourself a $50 cash dividend as a reward for your hard work.

Your new Retained Earnings balance becomes:

$126 (Beginning) + $150 (Net Profit) - $50 (Dividends) = $226
That $226 sits permanently on your Balance Sheet as accumulated, reinvested wealth.

3. Treasury Stock (Contra-Equity)
Treasury Stock represents money spent by the company to buy back its own ownership shares from investors.

Later in the summer, your stand has plenty of cash in the register. You decide to spend $20 of the stand's cash to buy back half of your partner's stake so you regain greater control over the business.

* Treasury Stock is a Contra-Equity Account, meaning it directly reduces Total Equity because company cash was used to repurchase ownership.

* It is recorded as a negative balance (-$20) in the Equity section.

Total Equity Summary Statement (Month 2 Snapshot)
Putting all three pieces together at the end of Month 2:

* Share Capital: $100 (Partner's initial investment)
* Retained Earnings: $226 (Accumulated profits after dividends)
* Treasury Stock: ($20) (Cost of bought-back shares)
Total Equity = $100 + $226 - $20 = $306
Verified Balance Sheet Equation
Assets = Liabilities + Equity

If your lemonade stand now holds $846 in total assets (cash, squeezer, inventory) and still owes $540 in total liabilities (supplier bills and initial loan):

$846 (Assets) = $540 (Liabilities) + $306 (Equity)

The equation holds true: every single dollar of assets on your stand is financed either by lenders ($540) or by your accumulated owner's equity ($306).

The Accounting Equation

Before you pour your first cup of lemonade for the day, you verify the golden rule of finance. A Balance Sheet must always balance:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
$666 = $540 + $126

It matches down to the penny. Every single asset sitting at your lemonade stand was funded by either someone else's money (Liabilities) or your own generated earnings (Equity).

Financial Health Check & Ratio Analysis

Looking at these numbers gives you instant answers about how healthy your stand is:

* Current Ratio
* Formula: Current Assets / Current Liabilities
* Calculation: $466 / $40 = 11.65x
* What It Means: Liquidity = Measures your ability to cover short-term bills. Having $11.65 in quick cash, receivables, and stock for every $1.00 of immediate debt means you won't be caught short.

* Debt-to-Equity
* Formula: Total Liabilities / Total Equity
* Calculation: $540 / $126 = 4.29x
* What It Means: Leverage = Compares borrowed money to owner value. A higher number warns that your stand relies heavily on debt. Your focus in Month 2 will be paying down that $500 loan.

* Working Capital
* Formula: Current Assets - Current Liabilities
* Calculation: $466 - $40 = $426
* What It Means: Operational Buffer = The total dollar cushion available to fund day-to-day operations, buy fresh ingredients, and deal with unexpected costs.
